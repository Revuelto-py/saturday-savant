The drive is football's possession

{{ '{:,}'.format(n_games) }} FBS games in {{ season }}, every drive in them read

Points per game rewards fast offenses that simply get more chances. SVR measures points per drive instead, then scales by 10 — roughly one game's worth of possessions — so the numbers read like scores. A methodical offense that scores on half its drives rates identically whether it runs 9 possessions a game or 14.

Not every drive counts

{{ '{:,}'.format(n_drives) }} drives left after garbage time, kneel-downs and overtime

Drives that aren't real attempts to score are thrown out before anything is computed: kneel-downs at the end of a half, overtime possessions (which start in field-goal range), and all of garbage time — any drive beginning with the score out of hand for that stage of the game (up {{ gm[2] }}+ in the 2nd quarter, {{ gm[3] }}+ in the 3rd, {{ gm[4] }}+ in the 4th). That's also why SVR needs no artificial cap on blowout wins: a drive maxes out at 8 points, and the padded second half of a 56–0 game simply never enters the sample.

Pick-sixes and kick-return touchdowns belong to neither an offense nor the defense that allowed a drive, so they're excluded from both sides. Games against FCS opponents don't count at all.

Every number is opponent-adjusted

{{ n_teams }} teams solved against each other at once

Scoring 2.5 points per drive on the best defense in America means something entirely different from doing it against the worst. Each game's efficiency is rescaled by the opponent's own adjusted rating — and since those ratings depend on their opponents too, the whole system is recalculated over and over until every rating is consistent with every other.

Home field is taken out

{{ '%.2f'|format(svr.hfa) }} home-to-away points per drive, held fixed

Home-field advantage — worth about {{ ((svr.hfa - 1) * 100)|round|int }}% more points per drive, measured across seven completed seasons rather than the one in progress — is removed, so every rating is expressed at a neutral site.

It is held fixed on purpose: September schedules are lopsided by design, so a figure measured live in week 3 reads nearly double the truth and distorts every team that happened to open at home.

Built to predict, not to reward

{{ (svr.regress * 100)|round|int }}% of last season's edge carried in, worth {{ svr.prior_drives }} drives

Recent games count a little more than September ones, and early in a season every team's small sample is blended toward what that team was expected to be — last season's rating, pulled toward average — until the new season's games outweigh it.

That anchor is what makes the schedule adjustment bite in September: a weak opponent doesn't start out rated average, so beating them is discounted properly instead of lifting a one-game team into the top ten.

Net Rating
Offense minus Defense: the margin we'd expect against a perfectly average FBS team on a neutral field.

SVR answers one question: "if these two teams played tonight on a neutral field, what's the margin?" It doesn't care about résumés, rankings, or who deserved to make the playoff — which is exactly why it occasionally disagrees with the polls, and why that disagreement is informative.
