Savant Rating

Every team is rated against every team it played, and every team they played, until the whole country agrees.

The answer is a number for how many points a team's offense and defense are really worth, with schedule, pace, garbage time and home field stripped out.

Six steps, goal line to end zone. Select one to jump to it.

1

The drive is the possession that counts

{{ '{:,}'.format(n_games) }} FBS games read in {{ season }}

Points per game rewards fast offenses that simply get more chances. SVR measures points per drive instead, then scales by 10, roughly one game of possessions, so the numbers read like scores.

A methodical offense that scores on half its drives rates the same whether it runs 9 possessions a game or 14.

2

Not every drive counts

{{ '{:,}'.format(n_drives) }} drives survive the filter

Drives that are not real attempts to score are thrown out first: kneel-downs, overtime possessions that start in field-goal range, and all of garbage time, any drive that begins with the game already out of hand.

That is why SVR needs no cap on blowout wins. A drive maxes out at 8 points, and the padded second half of a 56–0 game never enters the sample. Pick-sixes and return touchdowns count for neither side, and games against FCS opponents do not count at all.

3

Every number is opponent-adjusted

{{ n_teams }} teams solved together

Scoring 2.5 points per drive on the best defense in America means something different from doing it against the worst. Each game is rescaled by the opponent's own adjusted rating.

Those ratings depend on their opponents too, so the whole system is recalculated again and again until every rating agrees with every other. The web at the top of the page is the input, every game played. This chart is the output: all {{ n_teams }} teams, solved at once.

4

Home field is taken out

{{ '%.2f'|format(svr.hfa) }} home-to-away, points per drive

Home teams score about {{ hfa_pct }}% more points per drive. That is divided out of every game, so each rating is expressed at a neutral site.

The figure is measured over seven completed seasons and held fixed on purpose. September schedules are lopsided by design, and a figure measured live in week 3 reads nearly double the truth, distorting every team that happened to open at home.

5

Built to predict, not to reward

{{ regress_pct }}% of last season's edge carried in

Early in a season every team's sample is small, so it is blended toward what that team was expected to be: last season's rating, pulled {{ 100 - regress_pct }}% toward average and worth {{ svr.prior_drives }} drives. The new season's games outweigh it within weeks.

That anchor is what makes the schedule adjustment bite in September. A weak opponent does not start out rated average, so beating them is discounted properly instead of lifting a one-game team into the top ten.

6

What comes out the end

Offensive Rating
Points scored per 10 drives, adjusted for every defense faced.{% if avg_off %} This season's FBS average is {{ '%.1f'|format(avg_off) }}.{% endif %}
Defensive Rating
Points allowed per 10 drives, adjusted for every offense faced. Lower is better.
Net Rating
Offense minus defense: the expected margin against an average FBS team on a neutral field.

Net Rating answers one question: if two teams played tonight on a neutral field, what is the margin? It does not care about résumés, rankings or who deserved a playoff spot, which is why it sometimes disagrees with the polls, and why that disagreement is worth reading.
